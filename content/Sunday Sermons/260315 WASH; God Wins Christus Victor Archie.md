# The Cross, Christus Victor, and Victory Over the Empire

## Core theological theme

The sermon presents the cross through the lens of **Christus Victor**: Jesus’ crucifixion is not a defeat but God’s decisive victory over empires, unseen powers, sin, and death. The cross, originally a Roman tool of fear and control, is reinterpreted as God’s public proclamation of triumph over every power that enslaves humanity.

---

## Fear propaganda, empires, and the cross

- The sermon begins with a World War II Nazi leaflet posted in occupied Paris (August 22, 1941). 
  - The notice states that:
    - All detained French persons will be regarded as hostages.
    - In the event of further attacks against German forces, a number of hostages “corresponding to the seriousness of the crime” will be shot. 
  - This is identified as **fear propaganda**, designed to crush even the slightest thought of rebellion among occupied people. 
- The sermon notes that Nazis did more than hang posters:
  - They carried out public executions by hanging or firing squad across occupied territories (France, Poland, Czechoslovakia, etc.). 
- This tactic is placed in a long line of historical empires:
  - Assyrians, Persians, Egyptians, Mongols, Ottomans, Spanish, British, and even tongue‑in‑cheek references like the “Galactic Republic” and the Dodgers. 
  - Across history, empires have leveraged fear propaganda to maintain power and suppress resistance.
  - The message is consistently: **“Resist the empire and be crushed.”** 

- The sermon locates Jesus’ death within this pattern:
  - At the time of Jesus, the **Roman Empire** ruled through military strength and technological advancement.
  - Rome’s primary form of fear propaganda was **crucifixion**—the cross. 

---

## Roman crucifixion as empire’s victory proclamation

- The sermon quotes N. T. Wright to explain crucifixion:
  - Crucifixion was a central way for ancient authorities to show subject peoples “who was in charge” and to break any spirit of resistance.
  - It was “the sharpest and nastiest way” to assert absolute power and guarantee the victim’s absolute degradation.
  - It was not merely execution but deliberately designed for maximum humiliation. 
- Crucifixion logistics:
  - Victims were stripped naked and publicly displayed.
  - Crosses were placed:
    - On busy roads.
    - Just outside city walls.
    - On hills where everyone could see.
  - This placement mirrored how a modern business chooses high‑visibility spots for billboards; the cross was Rome’s **billboard of terror**. 
  - Death by crucifixion came slowly by suffocation:
    - Victims had to push/pull themselves up to breathe.
    - Over time, exhaustion prevented them from lifting their bodies, and they suffocated.
    - The process could take hours or even days, ensuring prolonged public humiliation. 

### Spartacus and the Appian Way

- A historical example illustrates crucifixion as political terror:
  - 71 years before Christ, the slave revolt led by **Spartacus** was crushed.
  - In response, Rome crucified about 6,000 rebel prisoners along the **Appian Way**—a 120‑mile Roman road used by thousands daily.
  - Crosses were set up every short distance so that for 120 miles, travelers saw rebels slowly dying.
  - The message: **“Resist the empire and die.”** 

### Cicero on crucifixion

- Cicero, a first‑century Roman philosopher, described crucifixion as:
  - “The most cruel and terrifying penalty.”
  - Something that should be far removed from a Roman citizen’s thoughts, eyes, and ears.
  - Even the mention of crucifixion was considered unworthy of a free Roman citizen. 
- Implication:
  - Crucifixion was reserved for rebels and non‑citizens.
  - It was a **declaration** against any thought or impulse of resisting the empire.
  - It functioned as a **proclamation of victory** over rebellion.

## Reframing the cross: Colossians 2 and Christus Victor

- The sermon then examines how the New Testament speaks of Jesus’ crucifixion, particularly in **Colossians 2:13–15**:
  - “He forgave us all our sins, having cancelled the charge of our legal indebtedness, which stood against us and condemned us; he has taken it away, nailing it to the cross. And having disarmed the powers and authorities, he made a public spectacle of them, triumphing over them by the cross.”   
  [Colossians 2:13–15 (Bible Gateway)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Colossians+2%3A13-15&version=NIV)
- The sermon highlights:
  - The Roman cross was intended as a public spectacle of **Rome’s** power over rebels.
  - Paul claims that on that same cross, **Jesus** disarmed the powers and authorities and made **them** a public spectacle, triumphing over them.
  - The image shifts:
    - From Rome’s victory over a criminal.
    - To God’s victory over the very powers (seen and unseen) that claim to rule. 
- Central claim:
  - **“Christ is not a victim of the cross; Christ is victorious through the cross.”** 
- Roman soldiers’ reaction:
  - The Gospels record that after Jesus’ death—accompanied by an earthquake and darkened skies—battle‑hardened Roman soldiers, who had seen many crucifixions, said: **“Surely this man was the Son of God.”**   
    (Referencing Matthew 27:54, Mark 15:39, Luke 23:47.)  
    [Matthew 27:54 (Bible Gateway)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+27%3A54&version=NIV)
- The sermon stresses:
  - The cross, that ultimate symbol of imperial defeat, becomes the place of **God’s victory proclamation**.
  - Jesus’ work on the cross is portrayed as disarming and exposing the powers.

## Unseen powers and authorities in the heavenly realms

### The unseen realm (Ephesians 3 & 6)

- The sermon explains that the “powers and authorities” in Colossians 2 are further described in Ephesians:
  - Paul speaks of **“rulers and authorities in the heavenly realms”** (Ephesians 3).   
    [Ephesians 3:10 (Bible Gateway)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Ephesians+3%3A10&version=NIV)
  - He also writes:
    - “Our struggle is not against flesh and blood, but against the rulers, against the authorities, against the powers of this dark world, and against the spiritual forces of evil in the heavenly realms.” (Ephesians 6:12).   
      [Ephesians 6:12 (Bible Gateway)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Ephesians+6%3A12&version=NIV)
- The sermon notes:
  - There is an **unseen, invisible world** around, beneath, and all around the visible world.
  - This unseen realm influences events and experiences in our visible lives. 
  - Even non‑Christians acknowledge unseen realities through concepts like quantum superposition and entanglement (multiple dimensions and unseen interactions), suggesting that “there is more than meets the eye.” 

- Christian belief:
  - Much (not all) of what causes injustice, pain, and brokenness is influenced by the malicious intent of unseen powers and authorities—the enemies of God. 
  - These forces are larger than any single human villain (Hitler, Stalin, Pol Pot, etc.); they function like a **puppeteer** behind the scenes, seeking to enslave humans to selfishness, fear, and sin. 

### Balanced view of evil (C. S. Lewis)

- C. S. Lewis is cited to encourage a balanced approach:
  - Christians should neither be **naive** about evil nor **obsessed** with it.
  - The goal is awareness of evil’s reality while keeping hope firmly centered on God. 

### Invitation to Alpha for skeptics

- Recognizing that talk of invisible powers may feel “far out” or hard to believe, the sermon invites seekers to **Alpha**:
  - Alpha is described as a safe space to:
    - Have deep conversations about faith.
    - Ask questions that may be hard to voice elsewhere.
    - Journey alongside others exploring similar questions. 
- Whether or not one currently believes all this, the sermon asserts:
  - The cross **claims** that God is victorious over these unseen powers and authorities.
  - The cross is a victory over both visible and invisible enemies.

## Victory motif from Genesis to Revelation

The sermon shows that the Christus Victor theme runs from the first book of the Bible to the last.

### Genesis 3:15 – Head‑crushing promise

- After the fall in Genesis 3, God speaks of enmity between the serpent and the woman, and between their offspring:
  - “He will crush your head, and you will strike his heel.”   
  [Genesis 3:15 (Bible Gateway)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+3%3A15&version=NIV)
- The sermon interprets this as:
  - A prophetic word about **Jesus**, the ultimate offspring who will **crush the head of Satan**.
  - A clear declaration of future victory, not a tentative hope. 

### Isaiah 25:7–9 – Swallowing up death

- Isaiah prophesies:
  - “On this mountain he will destroy the shroud that enfolds all peoples, the sheet that covers all nations; he will swallow up death forever. The Sovereign Lord will wipe away the tears from all faces…”   
  [Isaiah 25:7–9 (Bible Gateway)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Isaiah+25%3A7-9&version=NIV)
- The sermon:
  - Connects “this mountain” to the hill where Jesus dies.
  - Emphasizes that God will **swallow up death forever**.
  - Notes the response: “Surely this is our God; we trusted in him, and he saved us… Let us rejoice and be glad in his salvation.” 

### 1 John 3:8 – Destroying the devil’s work

- 1 John states:
  - “The reason the Son of God appeared was to destroy the devil’s work.”   
  [1 John 3:8 (Bible Gateway)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1+John+3%3A8&version=NIV)
- This is presented as an explicit **mission statement** of Jesus: to **destroy** the works of the enemy. 

### Hebrews 2:14 – Breaking the power of death

- Hebrews explains:
  - Jesus shared in humanity “so that by his death he might break the power of him who holds the power of death—that is, the devil.”   
  [Hebrews 2:14 (Bible Gateway)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Hebrews+2%3A14&version=NIV)
- The sermon stresses:
  - Jesus becomes human **in order** to break the enemy’s grip on death through His own death. 

### 1 Corinthians 15:54–57 – Death swallowed up in victory

- 1 Corinthians 15 declares:
  - “Death has been swallowed up in victory.
    Where, O death, is your victory?
    Where, O death, is your sting?…
    But thanks be to God! He gives us the victory through our Lord Jesus Christ.”   
  [1 Corinthians 15:54–57 (Bible Gateway)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1+Corinthians+15%3A54-57&version=NIV)
- The sermon notes:
  - This is **taunting language** toward death—mocking its supposed power.
  - Victory is not just Jesus’ but is **given to believers** in Christ. 

### Revelation 5:5 – The lion who has triumphed

- Revelation gives a vision of the end:
  - “Do not weep! See, the Lion of the tribe of Judah, the Root of David, has triumphed.”   
  [Revelation 5:5 (Bible Gateway)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Revelation+5%3A5&version=NIV)
- The sermon uses this to show:
  - The Bible begins with a promise of victory (Genesis 3) and ends with a vision of the **Triumphant Lion**, Jesus.

## Where do you feel defeated? Fear propaganda in the soul

- After tracing the victory motif, the sermon turns to personal application:
  - “Where do you feel defeated today?”
  - It acknowledges that many feel:
    - Family tensions.
    - Strained relationships.
    - Marital distance or pain.
    - Physical struggles (personal illness, caring for aging loved ones, difficult diagnoses).
    - Professional challenges (career insecurity, layoffs, changing industries, workplace frustration).
    - Internal struggles (restlessness, anxiety, depression, cynicism, lack of meaning and purpose).
    - Financial stress.
    - Hidden addictions or behaviors they cannot shake. 

- The sermon then reframes inner struggle in terms of **fear propaganda**:
  - The enemy of the soul (Satan and unseen powers) is likened to an empire dropping leaflets into the heart:
    - Messages of hopelessness (“It will never change.”).
    - Worthlessness (“You’re too broken; God could never use you.”).
    - “Not enough” (“You’ve done too much wrong; you’ve blown it.”).
    - Disappointment in God’s timing (“God isn’t coming through, so He must not care or act.”). 
- Key idea:
  - Following Christ does not guarantee a life without difficulty.
  - It does guarantee that **God is with His people in the difficulty**, and that the most decisive battle—the defeat of unseen powers—has already been won. 
  - Victory over these powers means they have **no lasting authority** over those in Christ, and real freedom is possible.

## Jesus’ own view of the cross: John 12

- The sermon shows that Jesus Himself understood His death in victory terms:
  - “Now the prince of this world will be driven out. And I, when I am lifted up from the earth, will draw all people to myself.”   
    John explains that Jesus said this to show the kind of death He would die (crucifixion).  
    [John 12:31–33 (Bible Gateway)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John+12%3A31-33&version=NIV)
- The sermon emphasizes:
  - Jesus saw His crucifixion as the moment when:
    - The “prince of this world” is driven out.
    - All people are drawn to Him by His lifted‑up death.
  - The cross is therefore Jesus’ **chosen means of victory** over sin, death, and the devil. 

- An invitation is extended:
  - Those who have never entered a relationship with Jesus are invited to come to the “I Said Yes” table after the service to ask questions and explore receiving this gift of victory. 

- The sermon underscores:
  - Jesus’ victory on the cross is **truth**—absolute truth.
  - The tension is that this truth does not always align with day‑to‑day emotional experience.
