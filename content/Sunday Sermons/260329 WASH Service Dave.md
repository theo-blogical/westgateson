# The Cross, Rupture and Repair, and God Rewriting Our Stories

## Core theological theme

The sermon presents the cross as God’s decisive act of **rewriting** the human story of rupture, sin, and death into a story of repair, reconciliation, and new creation. This is framed through the theological idea of *recapitulation*: in Jesus, God re‑runs and rewrites the human story from the inside, healing the primordial rupture of Genesis 3 and every subsequent rupture in human lives and in all creation.

---

## Rupture and repair in our stories

- Many beloved Disney and Pixar films (such as “Finding Nemo,” “The Lion King,” “Bambi,” etc.) begin with the tragic loss of a loved one, often a parent, especially a mother.
- This recurring pattern is traced historically to Walt Disney’s own story: after the success of *Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs* (1937), he bought a house for his parents as a gift, but in 1938 an undetected gas leak in that home killed his mother, Flora Disney. 
- The loss of his mother left an indelible mark on Disney’s life, and stories of parental loss and personal tragedy began to show up repeatedly in Disney films. 
- These stories of tragedy strangely resonate with filmgoers everywhere and even contribute to Disney’s professional success, because they mirror the deep patterns of human experience. 

- Psychologists describe a relational pattern called “rupture and repair.” 
  - Rupture: a relationship is torn or broken by separation, abandonment, loss, or betrayal. 
  - Repair: the relationship is restored so that safety, trust, and attachment are renewed and even strengthened. 
- Rupture without repair leaves people broken, insecure, and unable to attach in healthy ways, drifting emotionally and relationally. 
- When rupture is actually healed through real repair, relational bonds can become deeper and stronger, and people grow in wholeness. 
- This dynamic of rupture and repair is present not only in Disney storytelling but also in every human life and relationship. 

- The sermon situates Easter and the cross within this pattern: the cross is described as the climactic act of God’s repair in the face of the deepest rupture. 
- The series title, “Long Live the King: Why Jesus Died and What It Means for Us,” frames this sermon’s focus on how Jesus’ death rewrites the story of rupture into a story of restoration. 

---

## Recapitulation: God rewriting the story

- The sermon introduces the theological term *recapitulation* to describe God’s work of rewriting human stories and the story of the whole world. 
- This idea is traced back to Irenaeus, an early church father (c. 130–202 AD), who taught that God is in the business of rewriting human stories, not only individually but cosmically. 
- Recapitulation means that in Christ, God enters the human story, re‑lives it correctly, and redirects its outcome toward restoration and life. 

---

## The first rupture: Genesis 3

- The sermon goes back to the beginning of the biblical story to show the first and foundational rupture: humanity’s rebellion against God in the Garden of Eden. 
- God creates the Garden of Eden, representing all creation, and places humans there to tend it, steward creation, and walk with God in faithful covenant partnership. 
- God’s intention is intimate relationship: for humans to be His people and for Him to be their God, with nothing between them. 

- In Genesis 3, humanity chooses rebellion:
  - The woman sees that the fruit of the tree is good for food, pleasing to the eye, and desirable for gaining wisdom; she takes and eats and gives some to her husband, who also eats. 
  - Their eyes are opened; they realize they are naked; they sew fig leaves together and make coverings for themselves. 
  - They hear the sound of the Lord God walking in the garden in the cool of the day and hide among the trees; God calls, “Where are you?” and the man responds that he was afraid because he was naked, so he hid. 

- This passage is identified as Genesis 3:6–10.  
  [Genesis 3:6–10 (Bible Gateway)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+3%3A6-10&version=NIV)

- This scene shows the first relational rupture:
  - Distance now exists between humankind and God.
  - Fear, shame, hiding, and blame enter the relationship. 
- From this point on, all humanity is born into this rupture. Things are not as they should be. 

- Psalm 51 is cited to describe this condition: “Surely I was sinful at birth, sinful from the time my mother conceived me.”   
  [Psalm 51:5 (Bible Gateway)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Psalm+51%3A5&version=NIV)
  - The sermon clarifies that this does not mean babies are committing conscious wrongs, but that every person is born into a broken reality that shapes every aspect of personhood. 

- Psalm 58 is cited to go further: “Even from birth the wicked go astray; from the womb they are wayward.”   
  [Psalm 58:3 (Bible Gateway)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Psalm+58%3A3&version=NIV)
  - This emphasizes that human life begins within an already fractured world and a bent condition. 

- Paul is quoted in Romans 8:22: “We know that the whole creation has been groaning as in the pains of childbirth right up to the present time.”   
  [Romans 8:22 (Bible Gateway)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Romans+8%3A22&version=NIV)
  - The sermon highlights that not only humans but the entire creation is groaning under the weight of rupture and longing for repair. 

- Every form of brokenness, fear, and shame is traced back to this primal rupture in Genesis 3:
  - Sin, selfishness, violence, and death enter the world through this event. 
  - All human shame, fear, regret, and longing for repair flow from this foundational break between God and humanity. 

---

## Human attempts at repair and their limits

- Humans instinctively recognize that something is wrong and try to fix it. 
- Human beings, made in the image of God, possess real power and potential, and this leads to the belief—especially in places of innovation like Silicon Valley—that any problem can be solved. 
- However, even in regions of tremendous technological and economic power, deep relational and moral brokenness remain:
  - Marriages fail.
  - Parents and children become estranged.
  - Individuals and communities still experience deep pain and need. 

- The sermon stresses that the core problem—sin and rupture with God—cannot be solved by human intelligence, creativity, or effort. 
- The power needed for true repair is not found in human capacity but in God’s power and willingness to heal, especially through the cross. 

---

## The cross as the decisive repair

- The sermon teaches that the cross of Jesus is the means and the moment through which God begins to repair the problem of rupture and rewrite the human story. 
- Fleming Rutledge’s work on the crucifixion is quoted to frame this:
  - In the primal event, Adam’s disobedience brought catastrophic estrangement and folly upon human history.
  - Redemption involves a radical reconstituting of human nature, not based on human potential but on the power of God. 
- The story of Adam and Christ is described as:
  - Incapacity, weakness, and rebellion on Adam’s side.
  - Power, potency, and saving initiative on God’s side in Christ. 
- All the various ruptures in personal lives are rooted in that singular rupture in Genesis 3. 
- The cross addresses not just individual guilt but the cosmic condition of brokenness, sin, and death. 

---

## Romans 5 and the reversal of Adam’s story

- The sermon turns to Paul’s explanation of the cross as the rewriting of Adam’s story:
  - “Just as through the disobedience of the one man the many were made sinners, so also through the obedience of the one man the many will be made righteous.” 
  - “The law was brought in so that the trespass might increase. But where sin increased, grace increased all the more, so that, just as sin reigned in death, so also grace might reign through righteousness to bring eternal life through Jesus Christ our Lord.” 

- This text is identified as Romans 5:19–21.  
  [Romans 5:19–21 (Bible Gateway)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Romans+5%3A19-21&version=NIV)

- The sermon emphasizes:
  - Adam’s disobedience made many sinners; Christ’s obedience makes many righteous—right with God.
  - Sin’s growth only highlights the superabounding nature of grace.
  - Grace now “reigns” through righteousness, bringing eternal life through Jesus Christ. 
- When Jesus died on the cross, the original rupture was repaired; the central barrier between God and humanity was decisively addressed. 

---

## The temple curtain and the end of separation

- The sermon recalls the Old Testament temple system:
  - God desired to dwell with His people, so the temple became the place of His presence.
  - People brought sacrifices at the temple to know with certainty that they were forgiven and could enter renewed relationship with God. 
  - The temple layout moved from outer courts to inner courts to the “Holy of Holies,” the innermost space where God’s presence was said to dwell above the ark of the covenant—His throne. 
- A massive, richly colored curtain (blue‑purple, embroidered with gold and Edenic imagery) separated the Holy of Holies from the rest of the temple, symbolizing the separation of God’s space from human space. 

- The sermon then cites Matthew’s account of Jesus’ death:
  - “When Jesus had cried out again in a loud voice, he gave up his spirit. At that moment the curtain of the temple was torn in two from top to bottom.” 

- This passage is Matthew 27:50–51.  
  [Matthew 27:50–51 (Bible Gateway)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+27%3A50-51&version=NIV)

- The detail “from top to bottom” is highlighted:
  - If it had been torn from bottom to top, one might attribute it to human action.
  - Torn from top to bottom signifies God Himself has torn the curtain and removed the separation. 
- The theological point:
  - There is now no separation between God and humanity for those in Christ.
  - People no longer need to go to a specific sacred building to meet God; God rushes out to meet people anywhere and everywhere. 
  - Any place can become a meeting place with God when a person calls on Him—and sometimes even when they do not, as God can surprise people with His presence. 
- This event marks the beginning of God’s great rewriting of the story, turning a narrative of distance and exclusion into a narrative of access and nearness. 

---

## How God rewrites the story: three core truths

The sermon identifies three core ways God rewrites the human story through the cross.

### 1. The story is rewritten in person (incarnation)

- God does not rewrite the story from a distance; God comes personally into the story in Jesus. 
- The theological term is *incarnation*: God the Son takes on human flesh and lives as a human being in our world. 
- John’s Gospel is cited: “The Word became flesh and made his dwelling among us.”   
  This is John 1:14.  
  [John 1:14 (Bible Gateway)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John+1%3A14&version=NIV)
- Philippians 2:7 is also referenced: Jesus “made himself nothing” (emptied Himself), taking the very nature of a servant and being made in human likeness.   
  [Philippians 2:7 (Bible Gateway)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Philippians+2%3A7&version=NIV)
  - This “emptying” means Christ set aside the privileges of His divine status in order to enter fully into human life and suffering. 

- The sermon stresses:
  - God does not remain aloof from human suffering; in Christ, God enters it.
  - Jesus does not merely understand suffering conceptually; He empathizes with it because He has experienced it. 
- A New Testament teaching is alluded to: believers do not have a high priest who is unable to sympathize with their weaknesses; instead, Jesus understands what they are going through. (Hebrews 4:15)  
  [Hebrews 4:15 (Bible Gateway)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Hebrews+4%3A15&version=NIV)

- A pastoral analogy is used:
  - Superman would be useless to Metropolis if he did not have powers, but he would also be ineffective if he never left the Fortress of Solitude.
  - Similarly, God is both powerful and present; He has come near and remains involved. 
- The key pastoral assurances:
  - God is **here**—present with His people in their pain.
  - God is **powerful**—able to act and save.
  - Both are true at the same time, and this is very good news. 

### 2. The story is rewritten to change us

- God’s purpose is not only to change external circumstances but to transform people from the inside out. 
- The sermon notes that often what is broken is not just the situation but the person: hearts, minds, character, and desires. 
- Paul’s words in 2 Corinthians are cited:
  - “And we all, who with unveiled faces contemplate the Lord’s glory, are being transformed into his image with ever‑increasing glory, which comes from the Lord, who is the Spirit.” 

- This is 2 Corinthians 3:18.  
  [2 Corinthians 3:18 (Bible Gateway)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=2+Corinthians+3%3A18&version=NIV)

- God is actively working by His Spirit to transform believers into the image of Christ:
  - Changing how they think.
  - Changing how they respond to pain.
  - Rewriting their identity and character over time. 

- The sermon applies this deeply:
  - Pain, shame, guilt, fear, regret, and even the sense of “never being enough” are not the final word on a believer’s life.
  - Jesus wants to transform these experiences and their hold on a person’s story. 

- A psalm is referenced where the psalmist says he was trapped in a pit full of mud or “quicksand,” unable to get out, and God lifted him out and set his feet on a firm foundation.   
  This echoes Psalm 40:1–3.  
  [Psalm 40:1–3 (Bible Gateway)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Psalm+40%3A1-3&version=NIV)

- Jesus later teaches not to build on sand but on a firm foundation—Himself.   
  This reflects Matthew 7:24–27.  
  [Matthew 7:24–27 (Bible Gateway)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+7%3A24-27&version=NIV)
- Together these images show:
  - Jesus pulls people out of unstable, destructive places and sets them on the solid rock of Himself.
  - God’s rewriting of the story includes deep, internal change, not mere external fixes. 

### 3. The story is rewritten completely (comprehensively)

- The sermon insists that there is no aspect of a person’s story that is beyond God’s ability to enter and rewrite. 
- There is no brokenness too deep, no situation too frightening or overwhelming that God cannot address. 
- Paul’s language in Colossians is cited:
  - God was pleased to have all His fullness dwell in Christ and through Him to reconcile to Himself all things, whether things on earth or things in heaven, by making peace through His blood, shed on the cross. 

- This is Colossians 1:19–20.  
  [Colossians 1:19–20 (Bible Gateway)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Colossians+1%3A19-20&version=NIV)

- This means:
  - Nothing lies outside the scope of Jesus’ reconciling work.
  - Every kind of brokenness can be touched and transformed by Christ. 
- The sermon states that Jesus heals and reconciles “everything,” and that He is actively rewriting every story to the depths. 

- A pastoral picture is offered:
  - Some brokenness feels like a coffee cup with a small chip—annoying but not catastrophic.
  - Other times, life feels like that cup shattered into a thousand pieces—seemingly beyond repair. 
- The sermon suggests that God may be “best” at putting broken things back together and making them more beautiful than before:
  - The testimony of countless believers through history is that God has restored them from places of severe shattering.
  - God rebuilds lives in ways beyond what people can imagine. 

---

## Death, resurrection, and new life in Christ

- Paul’s teaching in 1 Corinthians 15 is cited:
  - “Since death came through a man, the resurrection of the dead comes also through a man. For as in Adam all die, so in Christ all will be made alive.” 

- This is 1 Corinthians 15:21–22.  
  [1 Corinthians 15:21–22 (Bible Gateway)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1+Corinthians+15%3A21-22&version=NIV)

- The sermon emphasizes:
  - In Adam, humanity inherits death.
  - In Christ, people are made alive—this is the heart of the rewritten story. 

- Romans 6:4 is then referenced:
  - “We were therefore buried with him through baptism into death, in order that, just as Christ was raised from the dead through the glory of the Father, we too may live a new life.”   
  [Romans 6:4 (Bible Gateway)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Romans+6%3A4&version=NIV)

- Key points drawn from this:
  - Believers are united with Christ in His death and resurrection.
  - New life is not only a future reality (eternal life after death) but a present reality now; God begins rewriting the story in the present. 

---

## Baptism as a visible sign of the rewritten story

- The sermon describes upcoming baptisms on Easter Sunday:
  - A large tank of water will be placed at the front, and people from diverse backgrounds who have come to Christ will be baptized. 
- Though their stories differ, one thing is common: Jesus is writing a new story in each of their lives and bringing them out of spiritual death into life. 
- Baptism is portrayed as:
  - A visible sign of being buried with Christ and raised to new life.
  - A public celebration of God’s rewriting work. 

- Listeners who have already been baptized are invited to remember:
  - What Christ has done for them.
  - What their lives might have been like had Christ not intervened. 
- The hope is that this remembrance stirs worship and compassion, motivating believers to invite others who feel hopeless and believe their stories can never change.

## The deeper magic: C.S. Lewis and substitutionary love

### The Edmund story in *The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe*

- The sermon closes with a story from C. S. Lewis’s *The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe*. 
- In the story:
  - Edmund, in his brokenness, betrays his siblings.
  - He later realizes in horror that his treachery has real consequences. 
- The White Witch invokes the “deep magic” written at the dawn of time:
  - The blood of every traitor belongs to her.
  - Edmund’s life is forfeit; she has the right to kill him. 

- This “deep magic” represents the moral order of the universe:
  - Sin has consequences.
  - Treachery demands death.
  - Wrongdoing must be punished. 
- Everyone lives under this reality; it is not a bureaucratic technicality but woven into the fabric of existence. 
- The White Witch is portrayed as a legalist who knows the rules and insists they be enforced—and she is “right” in that Edmund truly did betray his family. 

### Aslan’s substitution and the deeper magic

- Aslan, however, knows a “deeper magic” the witch does not understand. 
- In a private conversation, Aslan offers to trade his own life for Edmund’s. 
- Aslan willingly walks into the witch’s camp:
  - He endures insults, humiliation, shaving, binding, and finally death on the Stone Table at the hands of the witch and her minions. 

- Lewis explains:
  - If the witch had looked further back, beyond the dawn of time into the stillness before time began, she would have read a different incantation:
    - When a willing victim who has committed no treachery is killed in a traitor’s stead, the Stone Table will crack, and death itself will start working backward. 

- The sermon's theological point in retelling this:
  - There is something deeper and more powerful than sin and brokenness.
  - At the core of reality is the very character of God, whose essence is love. 
  - Love is the highest ethic and truest foundation; the logic of divine love is that God Himself becomes a willing, innocent, substitutionary sacrifice for traitors. 
- This “deeper magic” in Lewis parallels the gospel:
  - Justice is real; sin truly has consequences.
  - But beneath justice lies the deeper foundation of God’s love, by which God willingly absorbs the full weight of justice on behalf of sinners. 

---

## Justice, love, and the cross

- The sermon weaves together:
  - The reality of moral law (justice, consequences for sin).
  - The deeper reality of God’s character (love, self‑giving, substitution). 
- The cross is presented as:
  - The place where God, in Christ, willingly takes the punishment that justice rightly demands.
  - The act by which God “writes a new story” for humanity and creation. 
- Therefore:
  - Death is not the end.
  - Sin is not the end.
  - Tragedy is not the end.
  - Brokenness is not the end. 
- The resurrection and the cross together proclaim that God’s restorative love has the final word. 

---

## Pastoral application: living into the rewritten story

- The sermon addresses common struggles:
  - Guilt over past decisions.
  - Shame and fear.
  - Broken relationships (including those personally damaged by one’s own actions).
  - Lingering regret and a sense of never being enough. 
- All of these are traced back to the original rupture of sin entering the world, but the cross proclaims that repair is now possible. 

- The call is to:
  - Bring all forms of rupture—personal, relational, internal—to God.
  - Trust that God is actively rewriting stories through Christ’s death and resurrection. 
  - Participate in this “re‑storying” by surrendering to God’s mercy and walking with Him into new life. 

- The sermon prays that:
  - God would remind listeners that He is in the business of rewriting stories.
  - God would put the world—and individual lives—back together for His glory and for the good of people. 
- The final hope held out is that God is presently, actively rewriting the stories of everyone who turns to Him, and that His love in Christ is the deepest reality underneath every rupture.
